Research prototype · graduation project in progress

FUSE

Formal Unification for Structured Execution: an agent paradigm for discovering scoped regularities from experience, composing what is already justified, and asking for the evidence that is still missing.

The question

Can a long-horizon agent learn the small operational structure shared across documents, tools, interactions, and failures—without treating a generated guess as permission to act?

FUSE separates proposing, deriving, certifying, and executing. A reusable rule may suggest what to try in a new environment, but only current local evidence can authorize the action.

Three core ideas

01 · Category Agent

Move beyond a fixed ontology

Instead of classifying every observation into a vocabulary chosen in advance, the agent revises task-relevant states, primitive operations, and relations from operational consequences. If one operation behaves differently inside a category, that category must split; if two labels have the same typed behavior, they may share a role without losing identity.

02 · Empirical category

Make experience composable and scoped

Experience is stored as typed transformations and relations with provenance, applicability, conflicts, and dependencies. The result is not an unstructured memory: known consequences can be derived, incompatible evidence remains visible, and an unknown becomes a specific observation or certificate obligation.

03 · Mathematical representation

Let structure reveal what to observe next

Objects, transformations, paths, and relations provide a language for composition, equivalence, transport, and missing structure. The expectation is practical: a representation should compress search, expose unsafe identifications, and turn uncertainty into a bounded construction or experiment—not add formalism for its own sake.

Two progress directions

FUSE studies how an agent can turn knowledge scattered across documents, interfaces, and experience into a small set of useful, composable regularities.

Its research advances along two connected directions: learning an empirical structure that an agent can act on, and using mathematical representations to guide what the agent should investigate next.

Direction I

Empirical structures for agents

An ontology offers a vocabulary for describing a domain. FUSE asks how that vocabulary should change when an operation behaves differently, two apparently identical states admit different futures, or a relation holds in one context but not another. Objects are grounded in distinctions that matter for action; morphisms describe executable changes and observations; relations constrain how those changes compose. Language proposes an initial account, while behavior and counterexamples revise objects, operations, and relations together.

Source-local knowledge remains attached to its evidence, while shared libraries organize scoped patterns that may help with unfamiliar tasks. A planner can retrieve a partial structure, derive its consequences, request a missing observation, and bind the result to the current environment. The aim is to discover sparse cross-source regularities that improve real tasks rather than merely accumulate a larger graph. Mathematical-representation search remains outside this direction.

Direction II

Representations that guide discovery

Starting from an empirical category, this direction asks which mathematical representation makes a problem clearer. A candidate decomposition, invariant, or compatibility condition can reveal which state distinction is missing and which experiment would resolve it. New observations then revise both the representation and the proposed construction.

The intended outcome is a useful explanation, design, or construction with an explicit route back to the empirical system. No single target category is prescribed, and no advantage over an equally informed general solver is assumed. The central question is whether representation-guided induction reduces the work needed to obtain a reliable result.

Current progress

The current work starts from partial ontologies and tests whether useful structure can grow through interaction rather than being specified in full beforehand.

Natural end-to-end behavior

Partial structure can support coherent progress from interpretation to action in controlled task settings.

Compositional extrapolation

Previously grounded operations and relations can be recombined to address situations not recorded as complete examples.

Local completion

Missing distinctions or relations can be identified and completed where the current task exposes a concrete gap.

Regularity accumulation

Validated local results can be retained as scoped regularities and reused in later tasks without treating them as universal rules.

Why it matters

Long-horizon tool use

Carry useful experience across tasks while rechecking the current resource, permission, and postcondition before action.

Heterogeneous workflows

Align partial knowledge from different APIs and implementations into reusable operations without erasing source-specific constraints.

Scientific workflows

Use mathematical structure to formulate the next observation, construction, or validation needed for a defensible method.
